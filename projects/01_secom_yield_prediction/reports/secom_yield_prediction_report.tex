\begin{abstract}
Semiconductor yield prediction from high-dimensional process data 
is challenged by severe class imbalance, widespread missing sensor 
readings, and failure signals distributed across hundreds of process 
variables rather than concentrated in a few dominant signals. While 
machine learning methods have been applied to this problem, few 
studies report cross-validated performance under class imbalance or 
provide sensor-level explanations that process engineers can act 
upon. This paper presents a comparative analysis of three machine 
learning models --- logistic regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost 
--- for yield prediction on the SECOM semiconductor manufacturing 
dataset, incorporating SHAP interpretability analysis, a controlled 
experiment on missingness as a predictive signal, and recall-oriented 
threshold selection motivated by asymmetric misclassification costs. 
Cross-validated PR-AUC results show that performance differences 
between models are within noise bounds given the dataset's 104 
failure samples, but XGBoost achieves substantially higher 
operational recall --- recovering 19 of 21 test failures, including 
a failure masked by competing sensor signals that Random Forest 
missed --- at the cost of a higher false-alarm rate. SHAP analysis 
shows that 237 of 446 features are required to account for 80\% of 
total feature importance, indicating that predictive signal is 
broadly distributed rather than concentrated in a small subset. This 
challenges simple feature selection approaches and suggests that 
yield failure reflects multi-causal process interactions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Semiconductor manufacturing consists of hundreds of sequential 
process steps, each introducing variation that accumulates across 
the wafer. Yield --- the fraction of dies passing final electrical 
test --- directly determines fab profitability, as each defective 
wafer that advances through the full process sequence consumes 
significant time, labor, and materials before failure is detected 
at end-of-line test. Early identification of at-risk wafers enables 
intervention before these costs accumulate, motivating the 
application of machine learning to yield prediction from in-line 
process sensor data \cite{shin2000machine,huang2023survey}.

The SECOM dataset presents the core challenges of real-world yield 
prediction: 1,567 wafer observations across 590 process measurement 
features with significant sensor dropout, and a 14:1 class imbalance 
in which only 6.64\% of wafers fail \cite{dua2019uci}. A naive 
classifier predicting pass for all wafers achieves 93.36\% accuracy 
while recovering zero failures --- exposing the inadequacy of 
accuracy as an evaluation metric \cite{davis2006relationship}. 
Failure patterns are distributed across process parameters rather 
than concentrated in dominant signals, meaning that standard feature 
selection approaches and single-metric evaluations obscure the true 
difficulty of this prediction task \cite{salem2018experimental}.

Few studies report cross-validated PR-AUC for SECOM --- most report 
single-split accuracy or ROC-AUC, which are inadequate for this 
imbalance level and subject to high variance given the small failure 
count \cite{presciuttini2024advancing}. SHAP interpretability has 
been applied to semiconductor yield prediction generally 
\cite{wang2024improved}, but rarely to explain specific missed 
failures or identify masking effects where competing sensor signals 
suppress failure prediction. To our 
knowledge, the hypothesis that missingness patterns carry failure 
signal --- plausible given that sensor dropout may correlate with 
process anomalies --- has not been systematically tested on the 
SECOM dataset.

The primary contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A cross-validated comparative study of three model classes 
for SECOM yield prediction, providing honest uncertainty 
quantification that reveals single-split results overestimate 
Random Forest performance by 0.079 PR-AUC.

\item SHAP-based identification of a sensor masking case in which 
Feature 31 suppresses failure signals from Features 59 and 103, 
together with evidence that XGBoost recovers this failure where 
Random Forest does not, consistent with gradient boosting's 
sequential error correction \cite{lundberg2020local}.

\item A controlled experiment testing whether missingness patterns 
carry failure signal beyond imputed values, finding no meaningful 
improvement (+0.0062 PR-AUC), supporting the median imputation 
preprocessing decision and suggesting limited value in missingness 
features on this dataset.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II 
surveys related work on machine learning for semiconductor yield 
prediction. Section~III describes the SECOM dataset and preprocessing 
decisions. Section~IV presents the experimental methodology. 
Section~V reports results. Section~VI discusses implications for 
semiconductor process engineering. Section~VII concludes.

\section{Related Work}

Machine learning has been applied to semiconductor yield 
prediction since the early 2000s \cite{shin2000machine}, 
with methods ranging from k-nearest neighbor fault detection 
\cite{he2007fault} to deep learning approaches for 
variable-length sensor data \cite{kim2019fault}. 
Cascading classification systems \cite{stich2020yield} 
and ensemble methods \cite{busch2022yield} have demonstrated 
improvements over single-model baselines, though most studies 
report single-split accuracy or ROC-AUC metrics that are 
inadequate under severe class imbalance.

Interpretability methods have gained traction in semiconductor 
manufacturing. Wang and Chen \cite{wang2024improved} applied 
XGBoost with SHAP for yield prediction and failure analysis, 
while Presciuttini et al.\ \cite{presciuttini2024advancing} 
demonstrated LIME-based interpretability on the SECOM dataset. 
However, these studies focus on global feature importance 
rather than explaining specific missed failures or identifying 
sensor masking effects at the individual wafer level.

Class imbalance handling in manufacturing ML has been addressed 
through oversampling techniques such as SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote} 
and through native class weighting in ensemble methods 
\cite{boyapati2024enhancing}. Recent surveys 
\cite{huang2023survey,zhao2026survey} identify class imbalance and 
missing data as the primary open challenges in semiconductor yield 
prediction. The present work addresses both challenges 
simultaneously while contributing per-wafer failure mode 
analysis and a controlled experiment on missingness as a 
predictive signal.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\subsection{Dataset Description}

The SECOM dataset contains 1,567 wafer observations from a 
semiconductor manufacturing line, each described by 590 anonymized 
process measurement features and a binary outcome label indicating 
pass or fail at final electrical test \cite{dua2019uci}. Of 1,567 
wafers, 104 (6.64\%) failed --- establishing a severe class 
imbalance ratio of approximately 14:1. Additional challenges 
include 538 of 590 features containing missing values at an overall 
rate of 4.54\%, with four features exceeding 90\% missingness, and 
116 features exhibiting zero variance across all observations 
(Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). Feature values represent sensor readings 
from anonymized process steps; no universal threshold exists for 
acceptable missingness or minimum variance, requiring deliberate, 
documented preprocessing decisions.

\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}

Preprocessing followed five sequential steps, each representing a 
deliberate research decision with documented justification.

\textbf{Step 1: Zero-variance removal.} Features with identical 
values across all 1,567 wafers carry no discriminative signal. 
Exactly 116 such features were identified and removed, reducing 
the feature space from 590 to 474. Near-zero variance features 
were retained.

\textbf{Step 2: High-missingness removal.} Features missing in 
more than 50\% of samples cannot be reliably imputed without 
introducing more noise than signal \cite{park2026data}. 
Twenty-eight features exceeded this threshold and were removed, 
yielding 446 features. Common thresholds in the literature range 
from 30\% to 70\%; we selected 50\% as a moderate choice and 
examine sensitivity to it in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}.

\textbf{Step 3: Median imputation.} Remaining features exhibited 
a mean missing rate of 1.6\% (maximum 45.6\%). Median imputation 
was preferred over mean imputation due to the presence of outliers 
from equipment anomalies \cite{riaz2025robust}. KNN imputation and 
MICE were rejected due to computational cost and unverifiable 
distributional assumptions respectively. We assume Missing At 
Random (MAR) and document this as a limitation. Feature filtering 
and imputation were applied to the full dataset before splitting; 
because these steps do not use outcome labels, leakage risk is 
small, although fitting them within each fold would be stricter.

\textbf{Step 4: Stratified train/test split.} An 80/20 stratified 
split preserved the 6.64\% failure rate in both sets, yielding 
1,253 training samples (83 failures) and 314 test samples (21 
failures). Without stratification, random partitioning risks 
substantially unequal failure rates between training and test sets.

\textbf{Step 5: Feature scaling.} StandardScaler was applied 
within the cross-validation loop --- fitted on training folds 
only and applied to test folds --- to prevent data leakage from 
test set statistics into scaling parameters \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}.

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}

Model performance is evaluated using Precision-Recall Area Under 
the Curve (PR-AUC) as the primary metric \cite{davis2006relationship}. 
Accuracy is explicitly rejected: a classifier predicting pass for 
all wafers achieves 93.36\% accuracy while recovering zero failures 
--- a result that appears strong but is operationally worthless in 
a semiconductor fab where each missed failure incurs the full cost 
of remaining process steps.
Formally, Precision and Recall are defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \quad 
\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\label{eq:precision_recall}
\end{equation}

where $TP$, $FP$, and $FN$ denote true positives, false 
positives, and false negatives respectively. PR-AUC is 
computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{PR-AUC} = \int_0^1 P(R) \, dR
\label{eq:prauc}
\end{equation}

where $P(R)$ denotes precision as a function of recall 
across all classification thresholds.

PR-AUC is preferred over ROC-AUC for this imbalanced setting. 
ROC-AUC incorporates False Positive Rate, which uses the large 
negative class (1,463 passes) as its denominator --- even 
substantial misclassification of passing wafers produces a small 
FPR, making models appear more capable than they are on the 
minority failure class \cite{davis2006relationship}. Precision and 
recall are both defined with respect to the failure class --- 
recall over actual failures and precision over predicted failures 
--- so PR-AUC directly measures how well the model identifies the 
104 failures rather than being dominated by the majority class.

Threshold selection is examined explicitly because the default 
classification threshold of 0.5 is inappropriate for severe class 
imbalance. In semiconductor manufacturing, false negatives --- 
wafers predicted as passing that subsequently fail --- consume all 
remaining process resources before end-of-line detection, 
representing the highest operational cost. False positives --- 
correctly manufactured wafers flagged for re-inspection --- incur 
only re-inspection cost. This asymmetric cost structure motivates 
lowering the classification threshold to prioritize recall, 
accepting additional false alarms in exchange for recovering more 
true failures \cite{stich2020yield}. The optimal threshold depends 
on fab-specific cost ratios not available for the SECOM dataset; 
we therefore report threshold-independent PR-AUC alongside 
per-wafer results at each model's selected operating threshold 
(Section~\ref{sec:operational}).

\section{Experimental Methodology}

\subsection{Models}

Logistic regression serves as the linear baseline. Its coefficients directly indicate 
feature influence direction and magnitude, providing interpretability 
absent in ensemble methods. Class imbalance is addressed via 
\texttt{class\_weight=`balanced'}, which weights failure samples 
approximately 14$\times$ relative to passes during training, 
preventing the model from optimizing exclusively for the majority 
class. L2 regularization with $C=1.0$ controls overfitting in the 
high-dimensional feature space (446 features, 1,253 training 
samples). All experiments use \texttt{random\_state=42} for 
reproducibility.

Random Forest is selected as the nonlinear ensemble baseline 
\cite{breiman2001random}, motivated by the hypothesis that yield 
failures are driven by multivariate sensor interactions that 
violate the linearity assumption of logistic regression. The 
\texttt{max\_features=`sqrt'} setting forces each tree to consider 
only $\sqrt{446} \approx 21$ randomly selected features at each 
split, decorrelating trees so that different trees find different 
patterns and make different errors --- when 300 trees are averaged, 
these errors partially cancel. Three hundred estimators provide 
sufficient variance reduction for this dataset size. Trees are grown 
to full depth (\texttt{max\_depth=None}) to assess maximum model 
capacity, with overfitting examined explicitly via train versus test 
PR-AUC comparison. Class imbalance is handled identically to logistic 
regression via \texttt{class\_weight=`balanced'}.

XGBoost is selected as the regularized gradient boosting model 
\cite{chen2016xgboost}, motivated by its 
sequential error correction mechanism: each tree is fit to the 
negative gradient of the loss with respect to current ensemble 
predictions, so samples with large residuals receive proportionally 
more correction in subsequent trees. This addresses wafer-level 
prediction errors rather than feature-level diversity as in Random 
Forest. Key regularization parameters include 
\texttt{max\_depth=4} (shallow trees preventing memorization), 
\texttt{learning\_rate=0.05} (conservative per-tree corrections), 
\texttt{subsample=0.8} and \texttt{colsample\_bytree=0.8} 
(stochastic sampling decorrelating trees), and L1/L2 regularization 
(\texttt{reg\_alpha=0.1}, \texttt{reg\_lambda=1.0}) penalizing leaf 
weights to limit model complexity in the high-dimensional space. 
Class imbalance is handled via \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight=14.10}, 
the training-set ratio of passes to failures (1,170/83), which 
scales the loss contribution of failure samples --- amplifying the 
error signal so subsequent trees prioritize minority class 
correction. Early stopping with PR-AUC as the stopping criterion was 
attempted but found unreliable: with only 12 failure samples in 
the validation fold, PR-AUC estimation was too noisy, causing 
premature termination at 13 trees. A fixed 200-tree configuration 
trained on the full training set was therefore adopted.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

Performance is evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation 
as the primary framework, with mean $\pm$ standard deviation of 
PR-AUC reported across folds \cite{kim2017feature}. A single 80/20 
train/test split exhibits high variance with only 104 total 
failures; cross-validation averages across multiple splits to 
provide more reliable estimates. All models are wrapped in 
scikit-learn Pipelines \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} ensuring the 
StandardScaler is fitted only on training folds and applied to 
test folds, preventing data leakage. Each test fold contains 
approximately 21 failures, consistent with the held-out test 
set size.

A single held-out test set (314 samples, 21 failures) is reserved 
for per-wafer failure analysis and SHAP computation. Per-wafer 
comparison identifies which specific failure modes each model 
handles --- a failure recovered by XGBoost but missed by Random 
Forest suggests that sequential error correction addressed that 
failure mode; failures missed by both models represent genuinely 
hard cases potentially requiring additional sensor data or 
alternative approaches \cite{busch2022yield}.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) via TreeExplainer is applied 
to the Random Forest to provide exact Shapley values for each 
wafer-feature pair \cite{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020local}. 
For wafer $i$ and feature $j$, the SHAP value $\phi_{ij}$ is 
formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
\phi_{ij} = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{j\}} 
\frac{|S|!(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}
[f(S \cup \{j\}) - f(S)]
\label{eq:shap}
\end{equation}

where $F$ is the full feature set, $S$ is a feature subset, 
$f(S)$ is the model prediction using only features in $S$, 
and the sum is taken over all possible feature subsets 
excluding feature $j$. Positive values indicate the feature pushed 
failure probability upward; negative values indicate the opposite. 
Global importance is computed as mean $|\phi_{ij}|$ across all test 
wafers.

A controlled experiment tests whether binary missingness indicators 
--- one per feature containing any missing values in the raw data, 
encoding sensor dropout as a binary signal --- improve yield 
prediction beyond imputed feature values alone 
\cite{boyapati2024enhancing}. XGBoost is trained on two datasets 
under identical 5-fold CV: preprocessed features only (Dataset A) 
and preprocessed features augmented with 538 missingness indicators 
(Dataset B). An improvement exceeding 0.01 PR-AUC is taken as 
evidence supporting the hypothesis that sensor dropout carries 
failure signal.

All code, preprocessed data, and model artifacts are publicly 
available at
\url{https://github.com/sudipkharel779-netizen/semiconductor-manufacturing-research}.

The fold-level standard deviations of Random Forest and XGBoost 
overlap substantially, precluding strong claims of statistical 
superiority between these models at this sample size. A Wilcoxon 
signed-rank test on the five paired fold scores would be the 
appropriate significance test; however, with only five pairs the 
smallest attainable two-sided $p$-value is 0.0625, so no difference 
can reach conventional significance. The high standard deviation of 
Logistic Regression ($\pm$0.0446) relative to ensemble methods 
reflects its sensitivity to which specific failures appear in each 
test fold --- a consequence of the linear model's limited capacity 
to generalize across heterogeneous failure modes 
\cite{moldovan2017machine}.

Both ensemble models exhibit severe train-test PR-AUC gaps under 
single-split evaluation (Random Forest: 0.7257, XGBoost: 0.7506), 
with training PR-AUC approaching 1.0 for both models. This 
indicates memorization of the 83 training failures rather than 
learning generalizable failure patterns --- an expected consequence 
of the small failure count relative to the 446-dimensional feature 
space. Single-split evaluation also overstates 
performance: Random Forest's single-split test PR-AUC (0.2743) 
exceeds its cross-validated mean by 0.079. Cross-validation 
confirms that the train-test gap is not specific to one split. 
Fig.~\ref{fig:pr_curves} shows PR-AUC and ROC-AUC for each model 
across the five cross-validation folds.

\label{sec:operational}

between models. In semiconductor manufacturing, the relevant 
question is not the average precision-recall tradeoff but how many 
of the 21 failed wafers in the test set were identified before 
consuming further processing resources.

Among 21 test failures, XGBoost recovers 19 at threshold 0.35 
compared to Random Forest's 13 at threshold 0.20. Critically, 
XGBoost recovers all 13 failures that Random Forest catches plus 
6 additional failures --- Random Forest catches zero failures that 
XGBoost misses. This strict dominance at the individual wafer 
level occurs despite XGBoost's marginally lower single-split 
PR-AUC (0.2494 vs.\ 0.2743), illustrating that PR-AUC and 
operational recall can diverge when threshold optimization is 
applied to imbalanced problems \cite{srisailam2025fault}.

Per-wafer SHAP analysis of the Random Forest identifies one of the 
six XGBoost-only recoveries as a masking case. In this case (test 
set wafer index 37), Feature 31 generates a strong pass-direction 
SHAP contribution ($\phi = -0.05$) despite being elevated at 1.947 
scaled units, while Features 59 and 103 generate weaker 
failure-direction contributions ($\phi = +0.02$ and $+0.01$ 
respectively). In Random Forest, trees that split on Feature 31 
encounter the same suppressing signal, and averaging across 300 
independently trained trees dilutes the weaker failure signals, so 
the averaged prediction falls below the detection threshold. 
XGBoost's sequential boosting mechanism fits each new tree to 
the negative gradient of the loss function with respect to current 
predictions --- this gradient is large for samples where the 
current ensemble predicts poorly, so subsequent trees implicitly 
allocate more correction capacity to hard-to-predict samples, a 
plausible explanation for its recovery of this 
failure \cite{chen2016xgboost}.


Fig.~\ref{fig:waterfall} illustrates the SHAP waterfall for 
Wafer 37, showing Feature 31's dominant pass-direction contribution 
overcoming failure signals from Features 59 and 103. XGBoost's 
19-failure recall comes at a cost of 183 false alarms (202 of 314 
test wafers flagged, 64\%) versus Random Forest's 52 (65 flagged, 
21\%). The operationally optimal model depends on the fab's cost 
ratio between missed failures and false alarms. If the cost of 
processing a failed wafer through remaining steps substantially 
exceeds re-inspection cost --- a reasonable assumption for 
high-value advanced node manufacturing --- the XGBoost deployment 
at threshold 0.35 may be economically justified 
\cite{mendez2026machine}. Fab-specific cost parameters required to 
quantify this tradeoff are not available in the public SECOM 
dataset.

\subsection{SHAP Interpretability Analysis}
\label{sec:shap}

SHAP mean absolute values computed across the 314-wafer test set 
reveal that predictive signal is broadly distributed across the 
446-feature space \cite{lundberg2017unified}. Exactly 237 features 
are required to account for 80\% of total SHAP importance --- far 
from the Pareto 80/20 concentration expected in datasets where a 
small number of dominant variables drive outcomes. This finding is 
consistent with the hypothesis that semiconductor yield failure 
results from genuinely multivariate process interactions rather 
than single-variable deviations, and has direct implications for 
feature selection: aggressive dimensionality reduction retaining 
only top-10 or top-20 features would discard the majority of 
predictive signal \cite{kim2017feature}.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/shap_beeswarm.png}
\caption{SHAP beeswarm plot for top 20 features. Each dot 
represents one test wafer. Color indicates feature value 
(red = high, blue = low). Positive SHAP values push toward 
failure prediction. Feature 103 shows clear separation between 
high and low values; Feature 33 acts primarily as a pass 
indicator.}
\label{fig:beeswarm}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:beeswarm} shows the beeswarm distribution of SHAP 
values across the top 20 features. Comparison between SHAP mean 
absolute importance and Random Forest Gini importance reveals 
complete overlap in the top-10 feature rankings --- all 10 features 
identified by SHAP appear in the Gini top-10, though ordering differs 
slightly. This concordance between two theoretically distinct 
importance measures increases confidence that the identified 
features carry genuine yield prediction signal rather than 
reflecting artifacts of either method's specific assumptions 
\cite{lundberg2020local}. SHAP is preferred for reporting because 
it provides directional information and per-wafer attribution that 
Gini importance cannot.

The beeswarm also reveals directional effects for the top features. 
Feature 103 shows clear separation: high values push failure 
probability upward, while low values push it toward pass. Feature 
59 shows a similar but more gradual relationship, with high values 
pushing toward failure. Feature 33 exhibits an asymmetric pattern: 
low values push strongly toward pass prediction, while high values 
show a weak mixed effect, suggesting Feature 33 operates primarily 
as a pass indicator rather than a failure driver. Feature 31 shows 
the reverse of Feature 103: high values push strongly toward pass, 
consistent with its suppressing role in the Wafer 37 masking case. 
These directional relationships are actionable for process 
engineers: elevated Feature 103 readings represent a candidate 
statistical process control signal where intervention before 
end-of-line test may prevent downstream yield loss 
\cite{wang2024improved}.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

To address the potential concern that reported findings reflect 
specific preprocessing choices rather than genuine data properties, 
we repeated the full preprocessing and XGBoost CV pipeline under 
three missingness threshold conditions: 30\%, 50\% (baseline), 
and 70\%. Feature counts retained were 442, 446, and 466 
respectively. CV PR-AUC results were 0.1797 $\pm$ 0.0241, 
0.1910 $\pm$ 0.0271, and 0.1916 $\pm$ 0.0342 --- a range of 
0.012 across all three conditions. This range is smaller than the 
within-threshold standard deviation at every condition 
(0.024--0.034), indicating that threshold choice introduces less 
variation than fold-to-fold sampling. The 50\% condition reproduces the Dataset~A baseline of the 
missingness experiment (0.1910); both analyses used a pipeline 
separate from that of Table~\ref{tab:cv_results}, so absolute values 
differ from the 0.2083 reported there, and comparisons should be made 
across thresholds within this analysis. Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} visualizes CV 
PR-AUC stability across the three threshold conditions, indicating 
that the reported findings reflect properties of the manufacturing 
process data rather than artifacts of specific threshold selections 
\cite{park2026data}.

\section{Discussion}

The primary limitation of this study is sample size at the failure 
class level. With 104 total failures across 1,567 wafers, 
cross-validated test folds contain approximately 21 failures each 
--- a count too small to distinguish model performance with 
statistical confidence \cite{zhao2026survey}. The overlapping 
standard deviations in Table~\ref{tab:cv_results} reflect this 
irreducible uncertainty: the observed PR-AUC differences between 
models are within the noise of the cross-validation procedure. 
Future work should examine larger proprietary semiconductor datasets 
or apply transfer learning across multiple fab datasets to build 
statistically reliable yield prediction models \cite{huang2023survey}.

The controlled missingness experiment produced an inconclusive 
result --- adding 538 binary missingness indicators improved CV 
PR-AUC by only $+$0.0062 (0.1910 to 0.1972), below the pre-specified 0.01 threshold 
and within the noise of the cross-validation procedure. This 
finding has practical implications: sensor dropout in the SECOM 
dataset appears predominantly random rather than failure-correlated, 
and the median imputation strategy does not appear to discard 
meaningful failure signal. This null result provides a reference 
point for future work on this dataset, although datasets with larger 
failure counts may have sufficient statistical power to detect a 
genuine but small missingness effect \cite{riaz2025robust}.

The sensor masking case identified through SHAP analysis has direct 
implications for process monitoring system design 
\cite{srisailam2025fault}. Statistical process control systems 
that examine individual sensors independently cannot detect failure 
modes where one sensor reading suppresses failure signals from 
other sensors. The observation that XGBoost recovers this failure 
where Random Forest does not motivates development of ensemble 
architectures that explicitly model sensor interactions and correct 
for masking effects --- consistent with recent directions in 
multivariate manufacturing intelligence research 
\cite{kim2019fault,hsu2021multiple}.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a comparative study of logistic regression, 
Random Forest, and XGBoost for semiconductor yield prediction on 
the SECOM dataset, incorporating cross-validated evaluation, SHAP 
interpretability analysis, and a controlled missingness experiment. 
Cross-validated PR-AUC results show XGBoost marginally 
outperforming Random Forest (0.2083 vs.\ 0.1956), though 
differences are within noise bounds given the dataset's 104 
failures. Per-wafer analysis shows that XGBoost recovers 19 of 21 
test failures, including a sensor masking case missed by Random 
Forest, at the cost of substantially more false alarms; the 
missingness hypothesis was not supported at this sample size. These 
findings motivate two directions for future work: a stacking 
ensemble combining Random Forest's lower false-alarm rate with 
XGBoost's sequential error correction to reduce false alarms while 
maintaining high recall, and replication of this analysis on larger 
proprietary semiconductor datasets where statistical power is 
sufficient to detect small but operationally meaningful performance 
differences.
